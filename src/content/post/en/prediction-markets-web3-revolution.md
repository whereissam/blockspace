# The Evolution of Prediction Markets: From Wall Street Betting to the Web3 Revolution

On the night of the 2024 U.S. Presidential Election, while mainstream media was still cautiously dissecting exit polls, data on **Polymarket**—a blockchain-based prediction platform—already pointed decisively toward a Trump victory. That night, Polymarket became the global epicenter of media attention. Major outlets like CNN and the BBC began citing its data, with phrases like *"According to prediction markets, Trump's odds have reached 78%"* appearing frequently. What was once a niche tool for the crypto community had suddenly crashed into the mainstream.

But this was just the tip of the iceberg. By 2025, prediction market trading volume surged past **$40 billion**, a four-to-five-fold increase from the previous year. This implies that, on average, every American had staked over $100 on the future. Polymarket received investment from **ICE** (the parent company of the NYSE) and **Founders Fund**; **Kalshi** gained backing from **Sequoia** and **a16z**. Both companies’ valuations reached the billion-dollar mark. Giants like **Binance**, **Robinhood**, and **Coinbase** also joined the fray, either building their own platforms or investing heavily in the space.

All this begs the question: Has the era of prediction markets truly arrived? Or is this another flash in the pan?

### From Underground Betting to Financial Mainstream

The history of prediction markets is far older than most realize. In 19th-century Wall Street, traders were already placing bets on presidential elections. This wasn't a clandestine activity but an open market behavior. The logic was simple: if you believed a candidate would win, you bought their "shares"; if you thought they would lose, you sold short. The market price reflected the collective judgment of all participants—theoretically more accurate than any single expert.

However, this rational mechanism faced its first severe regulatory crackdown in the 1980s. Regulators argued that "gambling" on election results could undermine the democratic process itself. Imagine if Wall Street whales could manipulate market sentiment—and thus voter intent—by placing massive bets. Democracy would lose its meaning. Consequently, such trading was banned.

But the ban failed to extinguish the human desire to forecast the future. In the early 2000s, prediction markets resurfaced in academic institutions and corporate research departments. The University of Iowa and George Mason University established research projects, while companies began using internal markets to gauge employee sentiment on project success. At this stage, prediction markets were no longer viewed as gambling tools but as **"information aggregation mechanisms."**

Academia held idealistic expectations. Economists envisioned that through market mechanisms, we could "buy" knowledge from those with insider information—a Pentagon official aware of an impending military strike, or a researcher privy to clinical trial progress. If these individuals could trade, their knowledge would be transmitted to the public via price signals. It sounded beautiful, but reality soon proved the gap between theory and practice.

In 2001, **Intrade** was born. It gained fame by correctly predicting Obama’s 2008 victory when few thought a Black candidate could win. Yet, Intrade shuttered in 2013 under regulatory pressure. A similar fate befell **Augur**, the first decentralized prediction market on Ethereum. Launched in 2015 with high hopes, it struggled with high gas fees and limited liquidity.

Interestingly, the founder of Augur later joined Founders Fund and led the investment in Polymarket. It’s a classic crypto-world tale: a founder who saw their first attempt fail, then threw their full weight behind a new team capable of fulfilling the original dream.

### Why This Time Might Be Different

The 2024 explosion was fundamentally different, driven by the convergence of three key factors.

First is the **transformation of the information environment**. We live in an era of unprecedented echo chambers. Algorithms like TikTok’s and LLMs trap us in tailored streams of information. In this landscape, people have a stronger urge to express opinions on non-standardized events. You might be confused by the complexities of the stock market, but you likely have a firm view on whether "Trump will attack Iran," even if that view is inaccurate.

**Leo**, the founder of [42 Space](https://www.42.space/), noted in an interview: *"The U.S. election is essentially a sporting event."* This isn't a slight against democracy but a revelation of reality. In terms of capital, campaigning, and tribalism, an election mirrors the Super Bowl. Every "player" has loyal fans, and the historical data is vast. For the public, it’s simply "my team vs. your team."

Second is **Hyper-Gamblification**. Decades of monetary easing have pushed a new generation away from traditional investments. Young users on Robinhood, Kalshi, and Coinbase aren't satisfied with buying index funds and waiting for retirement. They crave participation, instant feedback, and the thrill of seeing their judgment manifest in real-time. Traditional investing is too slow, too stable, and frankly, unappealing to a 25-year-old.

Finally, **infrastructure has matured**. Ethereum Layer 2s have reduced transaction costs to negligible levels. A trade on Polygon costs mere cents, allowing users to frequently adjust positions without fees eating their profits. The prevalence of the stablecoin **USDC** allows non-crypto users to participate easily. You don't need to understand private keys; you just need to top up with dollars.

Perhaps most importantly, **regulatory attitudes are shifting**. In 2024, the CFTC allowed Kalshi to offer election-related products. This was a historic breakthrough. While Polymarket remains restricted for U.S. users due to compliance, the overall wind has changed. Governments are realizing that instead of banning these markets, it’s better to bring them into a regulatory framework to manage risk.

### The Ghost of Sports Betting

Walk into any prediction platform today, and you’ll find that nearly 90% of volume is concentrated in sports—NBA, NFL, EPL, etc. This puzzles many: aren't these markets for predicting politics, tech, or the economy? Why did they become a variant of sports betting?

Leo explains that the business model relies on transaction fees. Like any exchange, they need massive, consistent volume. Political elections happen once every four years; news events are unpredictable. Only sports provide a steady, weekly cadence of events with predictable volume.

However, there is a deeper conflict: prediction markets are competing with incumbents like **DraftKings** and **FanDuel**. These giants have spent decades refining pricing models, building user bases, and navigating regulations. Their odds adjust instantly to game changes—a level of precision new platforms struggle to match.

Furthermore, traditional giants possess massive historical data and professional analytical teams. An athlete's health, training data, and tactical nuances are all parameterized. Specialized funds hire data scientists to build mature models. For a newcomer, gaining an edge in this arena is extraordinarily difficult.

So, where is the advantage? Theoretically, prediction markets offer **decentralized pricing**. In traditional betting, the house sets the odds (centralized). On Polymarket or Kalshi, anyone can provide liquidity, which should—in theory—produce more competitive prices.

Yet, Leo observes that reality isn't so idealistic. Markets remain highly dependent on a few professional market makers. **SIG** (Susquehanna International Group), a giant in options market making, is now a primary provider for Kalshi. These institutions hold vast information and technical advantages, meaning the "decentralization" of the market is quite limited.

This obsession with sports exposes a fundamental contradiction: **Vitalik Buterin** once hoped prediction markets would combat social media misinformation. If a claim could be quantified and bet upon, lies would have a cost, and truth would emerge. But the reality is that only events that are easily quantifiable, rich in data, and hedgeable attract liquidity. Truly complex events—the ones requiring the "wisdom of the crowd"—often struggle for expression.

### The Paradox of Insider Trading

In January 2025, a mysterious account on Polymarket wagered $30,000 that the U.S. would take military action against Venezuela within 48 hours. At the time, the odds were just 5%. The next day, U.S. special forces raided a hideout belonging to Maduro, and the account netted over $400,000.

Was this insider trading? Almost certainly. But in the world of prediction markets, is this a sin or a feature?

Academic designs in the 2000s actually anticipated this. In fact, inviting insiders to trade was part of the original intent. The logic: why would a Pentagon official leak a secret? Only if they can profit. A prediction market provides that channel. When a massive buy order pushes the probability of a strike higher, other observers realize something is happening.

It sounds elegant, but it ignores a crucial question: **Who pays for this?**

In academic models, research institutions foot the bill. In the real world, the cost is borne by other market participants—meaning the insider's profit comes from the losses of market makers or uninformed traders.

For market makers, this is a nightmare. Even a top-tier firm like SIG knows less about White House movements than a presidential aide. This "toxic flow" is what market makers fear most. In standardized markets like stocks, there are mechanisms to handle toxic flow. In prediction markets, where it’s a "winner-takes-all" contract, the losses are direct and painful.

Consequently, for events likely to involve insider info, market makers either refuse to provide liquidity or set massive spreads (e.g., a 40% bid and 60% ask). With a 20% spread, a trader can almost never profit unless they have an overwhelming information advantage.

This is the **First Paradox of Prediction Markets**: They are designed to attract insider information, but the market mechanism itself repels it. As a result, truly important events lack liquidity, while data-rich events like sports dominate.

### The Temptation of Manipulation

If insider trading is a design contradiction, market manipulation is an operational challenge.

In November 2024, after Coinbase released its Q3 earnings, CEO **Brian Armstrong** posted a series of tweets. While they looked like standard corporate updates, keen observers noticed that every point he mentioned corresponded to a specific market on Polymarket. *"Will we launch options next year?" "Will users top 100 million?"* Armstrong’s tweets essentially provided the answers.

Is this manipulation? Legally, it's hard to define since CEOs have a duty to disclose. But from a fairness standpoint, it's an abuse of information. What if Armstrong or his associates established positions before the tweets? How is that different from traditional market manipulation?

The complexity lies in the fact that many prediction targets are **influenceable**. If there’s a market on *"Will Zelensky wear a suit before March 1st?"*, Zelensky’s team could bet on it and then simply arrange for him to wear one. They aren't just predicting the future; they are creating it.

Leo highlights a more subtle example: celebrity fan groups. If there’s a market for *"Will Celebrity X’s follower count exceed Y?"*, it becomes an arms race between fan bases who mobilize to follow the account while betting on the outcome. This isn't forecasting; it's engineering.

This is the **Second Paradox**: For high-impact events, the participants are often part of the event itself. Your bet might influence others' votes. A company’s management might adjust its product launch date based on market expectations. Traditional finance uses strict insider trading laws to mitigate this, but prediction markets essentially invite the "knowers" to the table. Where is the line? Currently, there is none.

### 42 Space’s New Approach

While Polymarket and Kalshi battle in the mainstream, **42 Space** is taking a different path. Its founder, Leo—a Stanford math grad and former Morgan Stanley trader—understands these dilemmas deeply.

Leo observed that current markets are tethered to external market makers, which forces them into standardized topics like sports and politics. Long-tail topics—like *"Who will have more Weibo followers first, Lu Han or Huang Zitao?"*—can't get liquidity because market makers can't model them. A Wall Street quant can model an NBA game, but they have no edge in predicting the behavior of Chinese idol fans.

42 Space’s solution: **Abandon market makers entirely and use a Bonding Curve mechanism.**

[Image illustrating the mechanism of a bonding curve in a prediction market, showing how price increases with token supply]

The concept is simple and inspired by meme coin platforms like Pump.fun. Every prediction isn't a "Yes/No" market but a set of tokens. For a mayoral race, each candidate has a token. Tokens are issued via a bonding curve—the earlier you buy, the cheaper it is. As more people buy, the price rises.

This design fuses the attributes of meme coins and prediction markets. Early on, it’s a game of speed and trend-following—ideal for degens. But as the event nears its conclusion, the market stabilizes. Rational investors adjust positions based on actual developments. Finally, the winner's token claims the entire market cap of the losers, and the others go to zero.

The innovation here is that **it doesn't need a market maker to start.** A niche event—like a prediction about traffic jams in Bangkok—can be launched by the local community. It doesn't matter if Wall Street cares; if the locals are interested, the market has liquidity.

Of course, this comes with a price. Bonding curves are vulnerable to MEV (front-running) and whales manipulating sentiment. But Leo argues that those looking for "quick money" and those looking for "accurate forecasting" are essentially playing different games in the same arena.

### The Oracle’s Curse

Every prediction market eventually faces one fundamental problem: **Who decides the result?**

For "Will Bitcoin hit $100k?", the answer is clear—check the median price of major exchanges. But for "Did the U.S. invade Venezuela?", it’s a legal nightmare.

When U.S. special forces raided Maduro's hideout in 2025, Polymarket faced a crisis. Was it an "invasion"? Experts disagreed. Some said no because there were no boots on the ground; others said any unauthorized military action counts. Polymarket eventually ruled "No," causing an uproar among those who bet "Yes."

This is the **Third and Most Lethal Paradox**: Prediction markets are not engineering problems; they are legal ones. Every market is a contract, and the power to interpret that contract determines the payout. In the traditional world, we have courts. In the prediction world, who is the judge?

Currently, platforms act as their own **Oracle**. Polymarket has an arbitration team; 42 Space is settled by its founders. This works for small scales, but can it sustain hundreds of millions of dollars? Some projects tried decentralized voting (like Augur’s REP), but those are easily manipulated by whales. Others use "decentralized juries" (like Kleros), but those are expensive and lack expertise.

Leo admits the Oracle problem is the hardest challenge—it’s essentially trying to build a rapid, reliable, semi-judicial system. It requires a blend of law, economics, and game theory that goes far beyond mere code.

### Who Is Harvesting Whom?

When giants like Ray Dalio and ICE invest in this space, what do they see? It’s not just the "democratization of wisdom." It’s the realization that prediction markets are a **zero-sum game.** For every winner, there is a loser.

Who is more likely to win? The institutions with information edges, supercomputers, and decades of risk management experience. When SIG enters the market, they are bringing quants to a knife fight. A retail user betting on "intuition" has little chance.

This is why traditional finance loves prediction markets. They package complex financial instruments into simple questions, enticing those without professional knowledge. It’s the same "democratization" play Robinhood used, which ultimately provided retail liquidity for high-frequency trading firms.

Leo puts it bluntly: *"They need retail because retail is the counterparty. They can harvest the ones with poorer pricing capabilities."*

### The Potential of the Long Tail

Despite the critiques, one possibility remains exciting: **The Long-Tail Markets.**

Imagine you are a sneakerhead. You and your friends argue over the next Air Jordan design. *"Will it use Flyknit?" "Will the sole be transparent?"* These questions matter to your community, but not to Wall Street. You want to bet, but you need a house and a judge.

Or you live in Bangkok and want to bet on whether the government’s new bus system will actually be finished on time.

These are long-tail markets: limited participants, but high engagement. 42 Space targets this. By removing the need for professional market makers, any community can create its own niche market. These won't have NBA-level liquidity, but they provide a channel for expression.

In these markets, the institutional advantage disappears. A ten-year Bangkok resident knows more about local traffic than a quant model. A hardcore sneaker collector understands Nike's design language better than a market analyst. Here, the "information edge" favors the user.

### The Mirage of Insurance

One of the most seductive visions for prediction markets is that they can serve as **insurance.**

If you live in California and fear wildfires, you could buy a position on "A major wildfire will occur this summer." If it happens, your payout covers your home repair. If not, you "lost" your premium but your house is safe.

Theoretically perfect; practically impossible. Prediction markets are zero-sum; insurance is not.

Insurance companies invest premiums in bonds and stocks. They run a massive portfolio where payouts are just one part of the cash flow. Theoretically, everyone can "win" because the money comes from broader market growth. Prediction markets don't have that. Your payout comes directly from someone betting *against* the wildfire. Who wants to be that counterparty? The risk is too high, and there is no "reinsurance" to absorb the systemic shock.

### Conclusion

As of early 2026, prediction markets are at a crossroads. They’ve had their "breakout moment," but the post-election volume decline has raised sustainability questions.

Leo believes if they stay on the current path, they will become boring—concentrating solely on standardized, hedgeable events. But he sees two diverging futures:

1. **Financialization:** Markets for commodities, earnings, and macro indicators. This will look exactly like traditional finance, dominated by quants and institutions.
2. **Communalization:** Long-tail, niche, and regional topics thriving on decentralized platforms. This provides a new way to interact socially—betting to express a view and using the market to reach consensus.

For the individual user, the advice is sobering: if you don't have an information edge, don't play. But if you view it as entertainment—a way to feel "skin in the game" for a topic you love—then go for it. Just know your place in the game. Are you the hunter, or are you the prey?
