# Stablecoins Today and Tomorrow: Market Structure, Revenue Models, and Risk Management

## The Rise of Stablecoins and Market Demand

In the cryptocurrency market, stablecoins have rapidly become an indispensable component. Over the past four years, the market capitalization of stablecoins has grown nearly fourfold, from $60 billion in early 2021 to $230 billion today. Investment firm A16Z has directly labeled stablecoins as the "killer application" of cryptocurrency, particularly during periods of high market volatility and limited innovation, when payment solutions and yield generation remain constant demands.

The essence of stablecoins is to maintain a 1:1 exchange rate with the US dollar. When a stablecoin falls below $1, market makers can purchase it to exchange for real dollars for arbitrage; when it rises above market price, they can mint new stablecoins using dollars for sale. This mechanism ensures that stablecoin prices consistently fluctuate around $1, providing rare stability in the cryptocurrency market.

The rise of stablecoins stems from two core needs. First is the global demand for US dollars—the dollar serves as the dominant currency in the cryptocurrency world, determining all trading standards. However, of the global population of 7 billion, only 400 million Americans have convenient access to dollar accounts, while the rest face difficulties when needing to settle in dollars. Second is the massive void in the Chinese market—as the world's second-largest market, China banned cryptocurrency trading after September 4, 2017, preventing the Chinese yuan from becoming a settlement currency for cryptocurrencies. This created an urgent need among Chinese-founded exchanges (such as Binance, Huobi, OKEx, etc.) for a settlement medium that was neither dollars nor yuan, a gap that USDT perfectly filled.

These Chinese entrepreneurs' exchanges became the earliest adopters of USDT, establishing its foundation as the leading stablecoin. With USDT setting the precedent, other stablecoins like USDC gradually began to develop, forming today's market structure.

## Stablecoin Market Structure and Operational Differences

The current stablecoin market has a total capitalization of approximately $240 billion, showing a clear hierarchical division. USDT accounts for 63% of the market share, about $149 billion; USDC holds roughly 20%, about $48 billion; while all other stablecoins combined represent only about 17% of the market, approximately $43 billion. This "cliff-like" disparity makes it difficult for newcomers to effectively challenge the leading stablecoins.

This structure results fundamentally from first-mover advantages and network effects. USDT has established trading pairs with Bitcoin, Ethereum, and other mainstream cryptocurrencies on major exchanges, making it difficult for new stablecoins to gain similar support. Unless a project is backed by someone of Donald Trump's stature, breaking the existing structure is nearly impossible. As industry insiders say, "If you didn't create a stablecoin in 2017, you shouldn't try now"—the window of opportunity has closed.

The stark difference in operational efficiency between USDT and USDC deserves deeper exploration. Tether (USDT's issuer) achieved a net profit of $13.7 billion in 2024 with just 150 employees, creating nearly $100 million in profit per person, and has become the seventh-largest buyer of US Treasury bonds. In contrast, Circle (USDC's issuer), despite having about $48 billion in circulation, generated only $1.7 billion in profit, with a team of over 800 people averaging only about $210,000 in profit per person. This disparity primarily stems from Circle's enormous investment in compliance, with its financial reports showing that employee and administrative costs consume 30% of revenue, approximately $260 million.

The revenue model for traditional stablecoins (USDT, USDC) is relatively simple: issuers invest users' deposited dollars in low-risk assets such as US Treasury bonds, earning about 4.5%-5% yield, but typically do not share this return with holders. This explains why Tether can be the "most efficient company in terms of profit per employee"—the interest generated from users' money is entirely captured by the issuer. This unequal distribution has catalyzed innovative attempts from new types of stablecoins.

## Innovation and Challenges in New Stablecoins

### From Yield Products to Stablecoins: The Rise of New Models

In recent years, a new type of stablecoin has rapidly emerged—yield products packaged as stablecoins. Ethena's USDe, for example, has successfully become the third-largest stablecoin, surpassing the veteran stablecoin DAI/USDS. This success stems from the market's increasing acceptance of the "tokenization" concept, allowing various assets and services to be packaged as tokens.

USDe's core innovation lies in opening high-frequency quantitative trading strategies to ordinary users. In the past, arbitrage strategies between exchanges and funding rate arbitrage on perpetual contracts were primarily enjoyed by professional institutions. Now, these institutions are willing to share some of their returns with ordinary users in exchange for larger capital pools and market share.

Ethena's basic strategy combines crypto assets with futures contracts for risk-free arbitrage: holding stETH (Lido's Ethereum staking token) to earn approximately 3% staking yield, while simultaneously shorting Ethereum in the futures market to capture funding rate yields (which can reach 7-8% during bull markets). Combined, these revenue streams can exceed 10% while remaining immune to Ethereum price fluctuations. This model performs best when market sentiment uniformly leans bullish, but when long and short forces balance, yields decline.

Notably, holding USDe itself does not generate returns; users must stake USDe to become sUSDe to earn yields. This design makes it essentially a yield product rather than a traditional stablecoin. As market conditions change, Ethena is also exploring new revenue sources, such as collaborating with BlackRock to launch the RWA fund BUILD, introducing US Treasury bonds as a revenue source.

### Overcollateralization Model: MakerDAO's DAI/USDS

Unlike yield-focused stablecoins, DAI/USDS employs an overcollateralization model, a more decentralized stablecoin design. Users deposit Ethereum worth $100, and the system mints approximately $50 of DAI. This overcollateralization ensures that each DAI is backed by sufficient assets, maintaining stability even during Ethereum price fluctuations.

DAI's greatest advantage is its high degree of decentralization, eliminating dependence on traditional banking systems and effectively mitigating bank failure risks. However, its expansion is limited by Ethereum's market capitalization and collateralization ratio, creating an inherent ceiling. As industry experts say, this path has been "locked"—you cannot use Bitcoin as collateral (due to lack of smart contracts), and other crypto assets beyond Ethereum have market caps too small to support large-scale stablecoin issuance.

MakerDAO recently upgraded the DAI brand to USDS, with the two being exchangeable at a 1:1 ratio. This upgrade reflects the project's attempt to break through development bottlenecks, although the market structure is unlikely to fundamentally change in the short term. DAI/USDS has also innovatively shared US Treasury bond yields through a "savings pool" mechanism, allowing participating users to earn annualized returns of up to 12%, a feature worth emulating by other stablecoin projects.

### Lessons from Algorithmic Stablecoins: The Luna/UST Collapse Case

Algorithmic stablecoins were once viewed as the future of stablecoins, with Terra's UST accumulating substantial market capitalization and being considered the most likely challenger to USDC. However, the collapse in May 2022 fundamentally changed the market's perception of algorithmic stablecoins.

UST's core mechanism involved mutual convertibility with the Luna token—$5 worth of Luna could be converted into 5 UST, and 5 UST could be converted into $5 worth of Luna. This design relied on Luna's continuous appreciation to support UST's stability, while UST offered a 20% annualized yield to attract users. The problem was that the source of this 20% yield could not be reasonably explained, ultimately proving to be a Ponzi scheme.

This event taught the market a profound lesson: if you don't know where the interest comes from, then you are the interest itself. After UST's collapse, algorithmic stablecoins have almost disappeared from the market, and investors have become more cautious about stablecoin yield sources. This explains why new stablecoin projects emphasize transparency and clearly explain their revenue sources.

## Risk Assessment and Investment Strategies for Stablecoins

### Risk Assessment Framework for Stablecoin Investments

For ordinary users, assessing the risks of stablecoin projects is crucial. An effective risk assessment framework should include three core dimensions:

First is revenue source transparency. The warning that "if you don't know where the interest comes from, then you are the interest itself" is an important adage in the crypto industry. Stablecoin projects must be able to clearly explain their revenue sources; non-transparent high yields may hide enormous risks. Unexplainable high returns often indicate a Ponzi structure that will eventually collapse.

Second is team background evaluation. Does the team possess relevant professional capabilities and experience? Is the founder's attitude humble and practical, or excessively arrogant? Does the project have a history of successfully handling crises? These factors directly affect the project's reliability and long-term development. It's worth noting that overly arrogant founders often lead to poor outcomes, with SBF of FTX and Do Kwon of Terra being typical examples.

Third is continuous monitoring and risk management. "You can be irresponsible to anyone, but you must be responsible for your money." High returns correspond to high risks, requiring users to invest time in following project developments and founder social media, monitoring community evaluations and capital flows, and regularly reviewing the sustainability of revenue sources. If you want investments that require no monitoring, traditional bank deposits might be more suitable, albeit with correspondingly lower returns.

### The Competition Between Titans: USDT and USDC's Market Dynamics and Development Strategies

The competition between USDT and USDC, which together control over 80% of the global stablecoin market, reflects not just a battle for market share but a clash of different development paths and business philosophies. This competition can be analyzed across four dimensions: geographical layout, profit models, risk control, and future prospects.

In terms of geographical distribution, USDT has leveraged its first-mover advantage and relatively relaxed compliance requirements to successfully dominate emerging markets in Asia, Latin America, and Africa. These regions face currency devaluation and foreign exchange controls, creating strong demand for US dollars with limited access channels, a gap that USDT perfectly fills. Particularly in the Chinese market, after cryptocurrency trading was banned in 2017, USDT became the core settlement medium for Chinese exchanges, cementing its unshakeable market position.

In contrast, USDC has taken an entirely different approach—prioritizing high compliance and transparency, targeting developed markets in North America and Europe. Circle's close relationship with Coinbase gives it higher credibility among US institutional investors, but this strategy has also limited its global expansion speed. Circle's recent filing of an S1 document in preparation for going public further strengthens its compliance image while increasing operational complexity and costs.

The profit models of the two companies show dramatically different efficiency levels. Tether achieved a net profit of $13.7 billion in 2024, with a team of just 150 people averaging nearly $100 million in profit per person; Circle's net profit was only $1.7 billion, with over 800 team members averaging only 0.2% of Tether's per-capita output. This disparity stems from three main factors:

First, the enormous difference in compliance costs. Circle's financial reports show that it spends nearly 30% of its revenue (about $260 million) on employee and administrative expenses, mostly for compliance and regulatory engagement. Tether's compliance investment is relatively smaller, allowing it to maintain extremely high profit margins.

Second, different channel subsidy strategies. Circle needs to pay substantial subsidies to exchanges like Coinbase to promote USDC, with some users even receiving 4.1% deposit yields. Tether offers almost no such subsidies, keeping all its earnings internal.

Third, investment strategy differences. Tether has adopted a more aggressive investment strategy, not only purchasing US Treasury bonds but also investing in assets like Bitcoin and gold, capitalizing on price increases in these assets. The strong rise in gold prices in 2024 brought Tether considerable additional returns. Circle primarily invests in short-term Treasury bonds, with returns that are stable but limited.

From a risk control perspective, USDT has long faced transparency concerns, but these worries have significantly diminished as the market has developed. After experiencing investigation and penalties from US regulatory agencies, Tether has notably improved its reserve fund transparency, now regularly publishing audit reports. Although the audit firms are not among the Big Four accounting firms, the improvement is significant. Simultaneously, its business model's extremely high profitability and leading market share position give it no incentive to risk damaging its reputation.

USDC takes risk control even further, with almost all its funds invested in extremely low-risk assets like US short-term Treasury bonds and cash, audited by top-tier institutions. This near-extreme safety strategy ensures its recognition in the institutional market but also limits its earning potential.

Looking forward, the competition between USDT and USDC may result in long-term coexistence. USDT possesses network effects in global payments and trade settlements that are difficult to replace, especially in regions with limited dollar access channels; USDC holds advantages in highly regulated institutional markets and financial products. For instance, blockchain project Humane AI chose USDC as its payment settlement solution specifically for its compliance in the US market.

As the crypto market further matures and regulatory frameworks improve, these two stablecoin giants may deepen their expertise in their respective domains while competing in frontier markets. Emerging markets like India, Southeast Asia, and Africa will become the next competitive hotspots, and whichever can establish first-mover advantages in these regions will largely determine the future global stablecoin landscape.

### Investment Advice for Newcomers

For investors entering the crypto space, a conservative strategy is essential. First, control your position size—don't invest too much in high-risk assets. Many people enter the crypto world through "meme coins," and while this "super-fast tutorial version of currency life cycles" can help quickly understand how the industry operates, investment proportions should be strictly controlled.

Starting with mainstream coins is a wise choice, especially foundational assets like Bitcoin. New users should be extremely cautious about leverage trading. Exchanges often engage in "mythical promotional campaigns" when attracting new users, but in reality, the profit-loss ratio in the contract market may be even more brutal than the traditional stock market's "one win, two break-even, seven lose" ratio.

A balanced allocation approach might be "half Bitcoin, half stablecoins." The Bitcoin portion provides long-term growth potential, while the stablecoin portion generates stable cash flow through yield strategies. This configuration helps balance mentality: when Bitcoin rises, the satisfaction of increasing total assets; when it falls, it can be viewed as an opportunity to increase coin-denominated holdings. Meanwhile, stablecoin yield earnings can be used for small-scale participation in emerging projects, maintaining market awareness without affecting overall asset allocation.

Additionally, it's advisable to diversify holdings across different stablecoins rather than placing all funds in a single stablecoin, to spread risk.

## Future Development Trends for Stablecoins

### Prospects for Traditional Finance and Stablecoin Integration

Traditional financial institutions are gradually entering the stablecoin market, but the integration of RWA (Real-World Assets) with crypto assets faces multiple challenges. First is the lack of competitive returns—crypto users expect yields far higher than those in traditional finance, making traditional assets insufficiently attractive. Second is the custody issue—non-standardized assets (such as farmland, real estate) are difficult to reliably custody. Third is the liquidity problem—RWAs struggle to achieve good liquidity in secondary markets. Finally, regulatory progress is slow—specialized RWA trading platforms face complex regulatory obstacles.

US Treasury bonds are among the few RWA assets successfully integrated with stablecoins; other asset types (such as real estate, corporate bonds, farmland) will struggle to achieve scaled development in the short term. As industry experts say, "If this were truly a good business, it wouldn't need to raise funds in the crypto space"—quality assets can obtain sufficient funding in the traditional financial world without requiring crypto channels.

This integration appears more like traditional financial institutions attempting to find new sales channels rather than an organic need from crypto natives. For crypto users accustomed to high yields and high volatility, the stable but relatively low returns of traditional assets lack sufficient appeal.

### Future Directions for Stablecoin Innovation

As the cryptocurrency world's infrastructure, stablecoins have evolved from simple transaction mediums into comprehensive financial tools combining payments, trading, and yield generation. Future development trends include:

Market structure stabilization. The dominant positions of USDT and USDC will be difficult to challenge in the short term, with new stablecoins primarily competing within the third tier, trying to capture market share from projects like USDe. This explains why we see more stablecoins imitating Ethena's model, such as DWF's USDF.

Diversification of revenue models. As market conditions change, the vulnerability of single revenue sources becomes increasingly apparent. Successful stablecoin projects need to combine multiple revenue sources, including funding rate arbitrage, US Treasury bonds, exchange partnerships, staking rewards, and DeFi protocol integration. When one revenue source declines (such as funding rates), others can compensate, maintaining overall yield stability.

Increasing compliance requirements. As regulation strengthens, transparency and compliance will become key competitive advantages. Circle's preparation for going public signals the stablecoin industry's maturation, and more projects may follow this trend, increasing financial transparency and compliance investment.

Deeper integration with DeFi. Stablecoins will play an increasingly central role in the decentralized finance ecosystem, expanding their influence through providing liquidity, participating in governance, and creating new financial products. We already see many new stablecoins actively seeking various DeFi scenarios, creating new use cases and value capture mechanisms.

## Conclusion: The Core Position of Stablecoins in the Crypto Ecosystem

Stablecoins have evolved from simple dollar tokens into innovative products combining multiple financial functions. They not only provide trading counterparts but have also become liquidity sources, risk hedging tools, and yield generators for the crypto market. In the highly volatile cryptocurrency market, stablecoins offer rare certainty, becoming safe harbors for users entering the crypto world.

However, users must remember that high returns inevitably come with corresponding risks, adhering to the principle of "being responsible for your money" to navigate this market full of opportunities and risks. As the industry consensus goes: if you don't know where the interest comes from, then you are the interest itself. Understanding stablecoin operating mechanisms, revenue models, and risk characteristics is not only essential knowledge for investment but also key to grasping the next wave of financial innovation.

The development of the stablecoin sector reflects the cryptocurrency market's gradual maturation, trending from speculation toward practical value. In this process, giants like USDT and USDC consolidate their positions, while newcomers like Ethena and DWF continuously innovate, collectively driving the field's development and progress. Regardless of how the market changes, stablecoins as the cornerstone of the crypto world will only grow in importance.
